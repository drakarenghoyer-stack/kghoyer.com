Understanding high blood pressure

It usually causes no symptoms, which is exactly why it deserves your attention. Here is what the numbers mean and what you can do.

Written by Karen Guimarães Hoyer, MD Sources checked · 2 October 2026

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries. When it stays too high over time, it puts extra strain on your heart, blood vessels and other organs.

Your clinician may call high blood pressure hypertension. It often develops without noticeable symptoms. Measuring it is how you find out whether it is high.

The numbers What your reading means

A reading has two numbers — for example, 120/80. The top number measures pressure when your heart beats. The bottom number measures pressure between beats. Both matter.

US adult blood pressure categories · mmHg
American Heart Association classification
Category Top number And / or Bottom number
Normal Below 120 and Below 80
Elevated 120–129 and Below 80
Stage 1 130–139 or 80–89
Stage 2 140 or above or 90 or above
Severely high Above 180 and/or Above 120

If the numbers fall into different categories, use the higher category. These are US categories; other guidelines use different thresholds. A clinician confirms the diagnosis from repeated measurements. See the action steps below for severely high readings. Source 1.

Make your home reading more reliable

Use a validated upper-arm monitor with a cuff that fits. Rest quietly for five minutes, support your arm at heart level and avoid talking. Take two readings one minute apart and record them. Source 6.

Why it matters The quiet damage

Over time, high blood pressure can increase the risk of heart attack, stroke, heart failure, kidney damage and problems with vision. Lowering it helps reduce these risks, even when you feel well.

What you can do Practical steps that help

Everyday habits can help lower blood pressure and protect your heart. Their effects vary, and some people also need medication. Choose a manageable first step and build from there.

01

Ease up on salt

Check salt or sodium on food labels. Choose lower-salt options and use less salt when cooking.

02

Eat more plants

Build meals around vegetables, fruit, whole grains and beans. The overall eating pattern matters.

03

Move regularly

Build towards at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, as appropriate for your health. Walking is one option.

04

Mind alcohol

Limit or avoid alcohol. Ask your clinician how drinking affects your blood pressure and treatment.

05

Manage stress

Make time for rest and manageable ways to reduce stress. Ask for support when stress feels difficult to manage.

06

Get help to stop smoking

Stopping smoking protects your heart and blood vessels. Support can make quitting easier.

Lifestyle sources: 3, 4 and 5.

Medication If your clinician prescribes it

Medication may be recommended because of your readings, your overall health risk or the response to lifestyle changes. It is another way to protect your health.

Two things that matter most

Take your medicine as prescribed, even when you feel fine. Do not stop it or change the dose on your own. If you notice side effects, speak with your clinician about the options. Source 6.

Red flags When to get help

Very high reading, but no concerning symptoms

If the top number is above 180 or the bottom number is above 120, wait at least one minute and measure again. If it stays this high and you have no new concerning symptoms, contact a healthcare professional as soon as possible. Do not change your medication dose yourself. Source 2.

Very high reading with concerning symptoms

A reading in this range together with chest pain, breathlessness, sudden weakness, difficulty speaking or changes in vision can be an emergency. Call your local emergency number immediately. Do not wait for the pressure to fall or delay the call to take another reading.

Sudden chest pain or signs of a stroke also need emergency help even if you do not know your blood pressure. Source 2; Source 3.

The takeaway One thing to remember

High blood pressure often causes no symptoms, so measuring it matters. Know your readings, choose a manageable health habit and agree a treatment plan with your clinician. Your target and treatment should fit your health needs.
